Unit 9 of 10

Tsientang University

A bottled-water fortune is building a new research university in the tea hills southwest of the city, designed to disappear into a forest.

In this unit you will learn
  • who Zhong Shanshan is and how a water company paid for a university
  • how a foundation became an institute and then a university in the making
  • the steps a new university has to pass in China
  • where the campus is, how big it is, and what the architects designed
  • who is building it, and when it is meant to open, and what your team's part is
Words to know
Foundation
A non-profit body set up to hold money and spend it on a stated purpose, such as a school.
Institute for advanced study
A research body with scientists and few or no undergraduates, modeled on the one in Princeton.
Master plan
The overall layout of a large site: where buildings, roads and open space go, before each building is designed.
Phase
One stage of a building project. Phase one is built first; later phases can wait years.
Photovoltaic (PV)
Panels that turn sunlight directly into electricity.
Atrium
A tall open space in the middle of a building, usually lit from above.
Local design institute (LDI)
A licensed Chinese design firm that turns a foreign architect's design into official construction drawings and takes them through government review.

9.1 The founder

Tsientang University (钱塘大学, Qiantang Daxue) is being paid for by one man: Zhong Shanshan (钟睒睒), the founder and chairman of Nongfu Spring, China's biggest bottled-water company, and of its parent company Yangshengtang. Nongfu Spring's red-capped bottles are in every shop in Hangzhou, and its headquarters is in the same southwest corner of the city as the campus, at 181 Geyazhuang in Xihu District; the institute that is founding the university gives the same address.

His story. Zhong was born in December 1954 in Zhejiang; sources differ on whether in Hangzhou or in Zhuji, the family's home town to the south. He left school after fifth grade, during the Cultural Revolution, and worked as a bricklayer and then a carpenter. When China's college entrance exam came back in 1977 he sat it twice and missed the cutoff both times, then studied Chinese literature at a television university, a correspondence college that taught by broadcast. In 1983 he became a reporter on the farm desk of the Zhejiang Daily, the provincial newspaper, and stayed about five years. In 1988 he left for Hainan, the island province that had just been opened as a special economic zone like the Shenzhen zone in Unit 4, where growing mushrooms and farming shrimp lost money. His first success was selling another company's health tonic as a regional agent. In 1993, at 38, he founded Yangshengtang in Hainan, a health-products company whose first hit was a pill made from turtle. In 1996 he came home to Zhejiang and founded the company that became Nongfu Spring, first named for Qiandao Lake west of Hangzhou, where its first plant drew deep, clean water. Its slogan, "Nongfu Spring is a little sweet," arrived in 1998; its sales passed 10 billion yuan in 2012. Yangshengtang also bought a struggling Beijing vaccine maker, Wantai, in 2001, and Wantai went on to make China's first home-grown vaccine against cervical cancer, licensed in 2019.

The money. Nongfu Spring listed on the Hong Kong stock exchange on 8 September 2020, and Zhong has been near the top of China's rich lists ever since. The Hurun list of October 2025 put him first in China at 530 billion yuan, a record for that list, with about 80 percent of it coming from Nongfu Spring. The company sold 42.9 billion yuan of drinks in 2024 from twelve water sources around the country, Qiandao Lake among them. Zhong seldom talks to the press; Chinese media call him the "lone wolf," and he has described himself as "a loner" who does not care what his competitors are doing.

On 22 January 2025, at Yangshengtang's annual meeting, Zhong announced that he would give 40 billion yuan (about $5.5 billion at the exchange rate of that month) over ten years to found a new university. Chinese press reports say he had the idea as early as 2016. He said the money would come from "Yangshengtang's accumulated resources and the contributions of every employee here." His stated aim is a "new" university "that China does not yet have": one that does "0 to 1" discovery in basic science and then turns it into technology people can use, "one leg in science, one in technology." The architects relayed the same idea in simpler words in February 2026: the university is "basically his legacy."

Did you know?

40 billion yuan is a lot even for a university. Westlake University, Hangzhou's other new private research university, was started with gifts from 36 founding donors, many of them entrepreneurs, plus land and buildings from three district governments; Fuyao University in Fuzhou was promised about 10 billion yuan by the glass maker Cao Dewang. Unit 10 compares them.

9.2 From a foundation to a university

A university in China cannot simply be declared. It grows through bodies that each need official approval. Tsientang has three so far:

  1. The Hangzhou Tsientang Education Foundation (钱塘教育基金会), approved by the city's Civil Affairs Bureau in May 2021. Its stated purpose is to fund a high-level private university. The money goes here first; the institute's website says the first 10 billion yuan has been credited to the foundation's account, with the full 40 billion arriving over 10 to 20 years.
  2. The Zhejiang Qiantang Institute of Basic Science Research (浙江钱塘基础科学研究院), set up with 100 million yuan from the foundation. Sources disagree on its start: November 2023 in one report, registered on 11 January 2024 in the city's approval records. Zhong is its legal representative.
  3. The Tsientang Institute for Advanced Study (浙江钱塘高等研究院, TIAS), formally registered in July 2025. This is the body the architects name as their client and the one that signed the construction contract. It calls itself "the founding institution for a new research-intensive university." Whether it is the earlier institute under a new name is not stated anywhere we could find.

What kind of university. The agreement between the city and the foundation (June 2025) describes it in four phrases: high-level planning, research-driven, internationally oriented, and "small but excellent." Its fields are science, engineering and work that crosses between them. TIAS lists four research areas: physical sciences, life sciences including biomedicine, AI and informatics, and humanities and social sciences. One commentary in University World News (July 2026) reports a plan of about 3,000 students in total, with more doctoral students than undergraduates, no traditional departments, and about 60 laboratories; that is a single source, so treat the numbers as the plan as reported, not a fixed design.

The first students. On 6 September 2026, TIAS welcomed its first 14 doctoral students, in a joint program with Zhejiang University: a year of coursework at Zhejiang University, then thesis research at TIAS, with a Zhejiang University degree at the end. Their fields are physical sciences, life sciences, medicine, AI and integrated circuits. The ceremony was held at TIAS's Longwu campus, its current home in the Longjing tea region, not on the new site. No date has been published for the first undergraduates.

The people. No president has been named. The best-known names are two physicists from Peking University. Chen Dongmin (陈东敏) is deputy director of the institute; he went to the United States in 1981 as one of the first Chinese physics students selected through the CUSPEA exam, worked at Brookhaven National Laboratory and Harvard, co-founded a photonics company in Silicon Valley, and returned to run laboratories in Beijing and Dongguan. Wang Enge (王恩哥) chairs the institute's Academic Committee; he was president of Peking University from 2013 to 2015 and a vice president of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The institute reports 82 staff, 21 named faculty, and more than a dozen "incubation projects" (its English and Chinese pages count 13 and 14), one of which is new kinds of specialized lighting.

Did you know?

The motto on the institute's website is three words: "Truth, Freedom, Gratitude."

Look for it in Hangzhou

TIAS's Longwu campus is in the Longjing tea region, in the hills southwest of West Lake, the same tea country as Longjing village in Unit 8.

9.3 The government's steps

Private money starts a university in China, but the city, the province and finally the Ministry of Education have to let it happen. Official notices still call the school 钱塘大学（暂名）, "Qiantang University (provisional name)." Here are the steps so far, in order.

The step that has not happened, as far as anyone has published, is approval by the Ministry of Education in Beijing, which is what turns a project into a university that can enroll its own students and grant its own degrees. When the Ministry listed ten proposed new schools in June 2025, Qiantang was not on it. That is why the first doctoral students are enrolled through Zhejiang University and why the name still carries "provisional."

Did you know?

Westlake University, whose first campus is in the same corner of Xihu District, went through the same ladder: a research institute first (2016), then Ministry approval as a university (2018), then its first undergraduates (2022). Unit 10 has the story.

9.4 The site

The campus is in the Zhuantang area of Xihu District, the hilly, forested southwest corner of Hangzhou, west of the West Lake scenic area and on the north bank of the Qiantang River. On the Unit 8.1 map, that is the far southwest, upstream from Qianjiang New City and the tidal-bore stretch of the river. Within a kilometer to the south are two arts schools, the China Academy of Art's Xiangshan campus and the Zhejiang Conservatory of Music; to the north and east is a golf course, and the forested hills of Xishan National Forest Park, beside Longwu tea village, are next door (the architects name the stretch by the site Wuchaoshan). TIAS describes the site as "beside Longwu Tea Garden," in the Longjing tea region, and the architects say the land was a tea plantation before work began.

How big. Sources do not agree, and the honest answer is that they count different things. The architects and their engineers say 475,000 square meters. The province approved 36.3 hectares, which is 363,000 square meters. The city's February 2026 planning notice counts 346,592 square meters of land across three phases. The institute's own site says "about 35 hectares." The larger figure probably includes land outside the built parcels, but no source says so. The floor areas in the planning notice are firmer:

Worked example

How big is a 346,592 square meter site?

  1. 346,592 square meters is 0.35 square kilometers, or about 35 hectares (about 86 acres).
  2. A soccer field is about 7,140 square meters. 346,592 ÷ 7,140 ≈ 49 fields.
  3. Central Park in New York is 3.41 square kilometers. 0.35 ÷ 3.41 ≈ 0.10, so the site is about a tenth of Central Park.
  4. Using the architects' 475,000 figure instead gives 67 fields and a seventh of Central Park.

The campus is about the size of 49 to 67 soccer fields, depending on whose boundary you draw.

Did you know?

"Tsientang" is an old spelling of Qiantang, from the postal system used before pinyin. The institute, the architects and the engineers all use it. Some famous Chinese universities keep old spellings the same way: Tsinghua University in Beijing is "Qinghua" in pinyin, and Peking University is "Beijing" University. Chinese newspapers write 钱塘, Qiantang.

Look for it in Hangzhou

A January 2026 visit found the site fenced in dark green barriers with excavators clearing the ground. Phase one has 893 days of construction in its contract, about two years and five months.

9.5 The campus plan

The master plan is by Renzo Piano Building Workshop (RPBW), the Italian firm behind the Centre Pompidou in Paris and the Shard in London, with the design institute of Zhejiang University (UAD) as the local design institute producing the official drawings. RPBW's partner in charge is Luigi "Gigi" Priano. Design began in 2023; the architects' first full design booklet is dated July 2025, a 75 percent construction-drawing set followed in July 2026, and the full set is due in March 2027. In April 2026 the Chinese architecture site Archiposition published the scheme under the title "Piano's plan under construction: Qiantang University, hidden in the forest."

Plots and phases. The site is divided into lettered plots. Phase one is Plot A, academic and public buildings, and Plot E, housing and shops. Plot C is phase two and Plot B phase three. Plot D is a golf course for now. The architects' booklet gives Plot A about 165,000 square meters, Plot E 55,000, Plot C 100,000 and Plot B 160,000; those four add up to more than the 475,000 square meter site, so they are not all counted the same way. The architects' 2025 booklet, which counts the later plots too, puts the full build at about 635,000 square meters of floor space, against the 399,492 in the city's notice.

A campus in a forest. The architects' big idea is that the forest is the subject and the buildings are the background. About 3,000 new trees are being planted. Near the buildings they stand in a regular grid, sweetgum (Liquidambar formosana) around the academic buildings and pagoda trees around the housing, planted at 10 to 12 meters tall and expected to reach 18 to 20 meters in about five years. At the edges the planting loosens into natural forest, which also marks the campus off from the regular street grid around it. The landscape borrows two ideas from the classical gardens of this region: jie jing, "borrowed scenery," and lou jing, the "leaking view" of light coming through leaves.

The buildings. Every building follows the same rules, which is what makes the campus read as one place:

  • No building rises above 20 meters, so they stay below the tree canopy; only the small meeting-room cubes on steel towers (below) reach above it. The whole campus sits on a plateau at 18 meters above sea level; the public piazza is lower, at 11.5 meters, and cars and trucks run underneath in the basement. The architects call the platform the "topos," a word borrowed from Greek for a place or ground: "a game between what we call the topos, almost like the soil, and the built."
  • Academic buildings are squares of about 54 by 54 meters with four or five floors. There are thirteen in phase one: nine standard laboratory and classroom buildings, plus four special ones, the library (A10), the auditorium (A12), the sports center and pool (A13) and the dining hall (A09). The research labs are underground, where they are quiet and free of vibration. Each standard building has a central atrium about 12 meters square with an open stair and a skylight at the top.
  • Ground floors are glass, lifted and set back, so the buildings seem to float. The base is very light local stone, the structure exposed steel, the facades low-iron glass with a second glass skin that works as a winter garden. The top floor is recessed and dark, which makes the buildings look lower than they are.
  • A glass photovoltaic canopy floats above every roof, with clear glass around the solar cells so the light underneath is dappled like light under trees.
  • Water runs around the academic blocks in shallow pools and channels 60 to 80 centimeters deep, with raised edges for sitting. A reflecting pool marks the main entrance.
  • Steel bridges link the academic buildings at the upper levels. Their decks sit about 5 meters above the ground and are planned in red resin concrete, a "fil rouge" (red thread) that marks the university's territory. One color is banned on the campus: orange, which the architects say is because it is Alibaba's color.

Along the piazza stand the buildings open to the public. The main piazza is about 80 meters across, paved in stone and kept clear for ceremonies, with shops around its edge. Around it: an auditorium with wood walls and red seats (the plans have shown 400, 478 and 600 seats at different dates), a library built around four stone cores and a central stair under a skylight, a sports center with a 25 meter, eight-lane pool under a skylight and a 40 by 50 meter hall for basketball, a dining hall with 684 seats, an exhibition hall and a faculty club. Plot E holds about two dozen housing buildings (the architects say 23 on their website and 24 in the booklet), each a 20 meter cube with a public ground floor and five floors of apartments, 395 in all, from 50 square meter flats for postdocs to 160 square meter ones for senior professors. Some 9 meter cube meeting rooms sit on steel towers above the canopy, the only tall elements on the site. The campus has no gates; it is open to the neighborhood.

RPBW already has one finished project in Hangzhou: OōEli, also called Tianmu Li, finished in 2020 after seven years of design and construction. Sixteen office buildings, most of them nine stories, surround a central park of 90 by 120 meters with a piazza, gardens and a shallow pool that can be drained for concerts and films. The same ideas run through the university plan: buildings of one height around a shared open space, with trees and gardens between them.

Look for it in Hangzhou

The campus plan shows 9 meter cubes on steel towers poking above the trees. Those towers are the only thing that will show above the forest from a distance.

Did you know?

The client's brief asked for a 50 meter, ten-lane pool. The design settled on 25 meters and eight lanes, under a skylight, below ground.

9.6 Who is building it, and when

The builder. On 30 December 2025 the Tsientang Institute for Advanced Study signed the phase-one construction contract with Zhejiang Provincial Construction Engineering Group, a state-owned builder, for a provisional 1.8 billion yuan. The city's notice gives phase one 893 days of construction, about two years and five months. In January 2026 the site was being cleared, and a project official estimated about three years to completion.

The design team is international. RPBW in Genoa leads the architecture, with about 20 people on the project. UAD, the design institute of Zhejiang University in Hangzhou, is the local design institute for the architecture, and Arcplus of Shanghai for the interiors. Knippers Helbig in Stuttgart, specialists in steel and glass structures, handle the structure and facades. The architects' July 2025 booklet also lists Arup (London) for mechanical and electrical engineering, ADCAS (Beijing) for the laboratories and Atelier Ten (New York) for sustainability; by February 2026 Arup was reported to be off the project. LYCS Architecture in Hangzhou manages the design on the client's side, with Peiyu Liu as project manager, and a Shanghai landscape architect is completing the landscape drawings.

When it opens. No opening date has been published. Inside the project, the campus is scheduled to open in June 2028, with most buildings finished as core and shell; the lighting proposal plans for substantial completion in August 2028. As of October 2026, Plot E's piles and basement slab were done and its walls were rising, while Plot A had just begun excavation around the piazza. On the design side, the architects' construction documents are due at the end of March 2027.

Did you know?

Every foreign design firm working in China needs a local design institute. The LDI holds the Chinese license, redraws the design in Mandarin and Chinese standards, stamps the drawings and takes them through government review. For this campus that is UAD for the buildings and Arcplus for the interiors.

Look for it in Hangzhou

Zhejiang University's main Zijingang campus is in the northwest of the city; UAD, its design institute, is the local architect of record for the new campus.

9.7 What the client wants

The client is Yangshengtang together with the Tsientang Institute for Advanced Study, and the person whose opinion counts is the chairman. He does not speak English; the project manager translates. The architects describe him as detail-oriented, quick to give feedback, then elaborating on an idea over weeks. He names no benchmark university; the architects say the Alibaba founder is his reference point. He wants the international team to present to him directly. The architects had never discussed lighting with him; asked whether he knows lighting, they answered, "He never asked."

What he has asked for, in his own words as the architects relay them:

  • Something "urban, at the scale of a person." Of the main square: "I want to feel like I'm in a European piazza, surrounded by retail."
  • Roots in Hangzhou: water, nature and respect for the land, "while being modern and to the highest standards," as Gigi Priano sums up the concept discussed with him. The landscape architect calls him "a very naturalist person." He prefers real nature, is sensitive to glare, wants the academic area quiet, and wants simple controls.
  • A pool, "always at the top of his list," and attention to well-being, arrival, streets and ground floors. He compares the residential piazza to an Italian village.
  • No gates. The campus is open to the public because he wants it that way.
  • "Excellent on day one," with no fixed brief: a flexible, mostly post-doctoral research university, and he is already thinking past phase two to phases four and five.
  • No orange anywhere.

The architects sum him up with one more phrase: "a low tolerance for low quality." His design director, Zhou Ying, wants the civic plaza open and social.

Did you know?

The chairman's presentation, with Gabe and Gigi presenting and about 15 minutes for lighting through a translator, was set for 1 or 2 November 2026, after your trip. As of the day you flew home it had not happened yet.

9.8 Lighting the campus

Buro Happold's lighting scope is the facades and public interiors of the phase-one buildings, the three piazzas, the bridges and walkways, and the towers. The forest, the paths through it, the retaining walls and the site roads belong to others, though your team would like to win back a concept role for them. In the academic buildings the design covers what a visitor sees: the atrium, the entrance lobby, the front circulation, the open side stairs and the perimeter outside; A08 stands in for the other standard buildings and A06, the main building, is designed on its own. The library, auditorium, sports center and dining hall get full designs. In Plot E, one residential type's public ground floor and facade. Stage lighting in the auditorium belongs to a theater consultant.

The narrative so far, in the architects' and the team's words:

  • "There's no monumental lighting in reality, it's more about tracing the roads between the buildings... lighting up what matters during the night, the ground floor and all the connections between buildings." (Gigi Priano, September 2026)
  • "Think about how the buildings go to sleep... it's almost like a little village. Some buildings... more 'awake.'" (Mario Giannini, September 2026)
  • "Lighting should be one of the few things that keeps everything together." (Gigi Priano)
  • Buildings as lanterns: the two working levels and the ground floor glow, the recessed top stays dark, so unlit tops read like unlit treetops and a tower "looks like it's floating above the treetops."
  • Stone lit by reflected light, not directly; the carved stone "belly" of the entrance building lit from below so it seems to fly.
  • Safety from evenness, not brightness: many low-output fixtures that keep people out of shadow, with brightness stepping down from the bright central plaza to the quiet academic buildings.
  • Bridges marked by small rhythm lights "like a lighthouse," with the light on the floor and the source hidden so faces read; the red deck risks turning people red under warm light.
  • The auditorium "like the inside of a violin," warmer than the rest of the campus; the pool diffuse with no visible source, because backstroke swimmers look straight up and the water mirrors every lamp.
  • Six priorities for the outdoors: wayfinding, identity, safety, flexibility, the right environment for each space, and places to rest.

The local rules. Chinese building codes come in three kinds: GB codes are mandatory, GB/T codes are recommended, and JGJ codes are industry standards; in GB and JGJ codes the clauses printed in bold are the compulsory ones. The ones that matter here are GB/T 50034-2024 (the national lighting design standard, the source of recommended light levels), GB 55016-2021 (the general building-environment code, whose light chapter is compulsory and which also limits outdoor light pollution) and JGJ/T 163-2008 (the night-scene lighting standard, which limits upward light). The local design institute says the campus must reach China's Green Building Two-Star rating, which drives the lighting controls (the team's own notes still call the level unconfirmed), and lighting power density is checked space by space with no trading between spaces. The landscape architect's advice on the hills: Hangzhou prefers a dark sky to the west, where the mountains are dark, so landscape lighting should be gentle at night and the apartments only a little brighter.

What the trip taught. Local practice runs brighter and whiter than the team's instinct: 4000 to 4500 K with a central bright fixture, against Gabe's preference for about 3000 K indoors and 2400 to 2700 K outdoors, and Mario's recommendation of 3000 K throughout. The local design institute wants international brands with factories in China, since imports take long to arrive, and the client usually buys the fixtures and makes the final call. In Hong Kong, Chinese fixtures sold at 10 to 20 percent of the expected price, and the team doubted the durability of many of the cheapest products.

Did you know?

Three Buro Happold groups split the buildings by color: RED for the auditorium, the library and Plot E; GREEN for the sports center, the dining hall, the main entrance and the towers; BLUE for the academic buildings A06 and A08.

Look for it in Hangzhou

Hangzhou has a lighting office that reviews the lighting of buildings on the main roads.

Check your understanding

  1. Who is paying for Tsientang University, and what business made the money?
    Show answer

    Zhong Shanshan, founder of Nongfu Spring, the bottled-water company, and its parent Yangshengtang. He pledged 40 billion yuan over ten years.

  2. Why are the first doctoral students getting a Zhejiang University degree?
    Show answer

    The university does not yet have Ministry of Education approval to enroll students and grant degrees, so the institute runs a joint program with Zhejiang University.

  3. Name the three bodies that have been set up on the way to the university.
    Show answer

    The Tsientang Education Foundation (2021), the Qiantang Institute of Basic Science Research (2023 or 2024) and the Tsientang Institute for Advanced Study (2025).

  4. What is the height limit for buildings on the campus, and why?
    Show answer

    20 meters, so the buildings stay below the tree canopy and the campus reads as a forest.

  5. Why do sources give different sizes for the site?
    Show answer

    They count different things: the architects' 475,000 square meters probably includes land outside the built parcels; the province approved 363,000; the city's planning notice counts 346,592 across the three phases.

  6. What does "no monumental lighting" mean for the design?
    Show answer

    Light goes on the ground floors, the roads and the bridges between buildings, the things that matter at night, rather than on the buildings as monuments.

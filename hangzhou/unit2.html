China's Land and People

China is high and dry in the west and low and wet in the east, so almost everyone lives in the east.

In this unit you will learn
  • why all of China runs on one clock
  • where China's 1.4 billion people live, and why
  • the plateau, deserts and plains that shape the country
  • China's two great rivers
  • the line that splits north from south, and what it means in Hangzhou
Words to know
Time zone
An area that sets its clocks to the same hour.
Plateau
A large area of high, fairly flat land.
River basin
All the land whose rain drains into one river.
Latitude
How far north or south of the equator a place is, in degrees.

2.1 One clock for a giant country

China is about 4,800 km (3,000 miles) wide, farther than New York to Los Angeles. A country that wide would normally use five time zones. Since 1949, China has officially used just one: Beijing Time, 8 hours ahead of London in winter and 7 in summer (China has no daylight saving).

One clock means the sun keeps very different hours in different places. In the far west the sun can reach its highest point in the sky as late as 3:10 in the afternoon. In Fushun, in the northeast, it can happen as early as 11:27 in the morning.

Did you know?

On any one day, the sun is highest in Kashgar about 3 hours 12 minutes after it is highest in Fushun, yet both cities read the same time on the clock. (The 3:10 pm is a February extreme, the 11:27 am a late-October one.)

2.2 Where 1.4 billion people live

At the end of 2025 China had 1.405 billion people. About 68% of them, 954 million, live in cities. In 2025 China had 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths, so the population fell by 3.39 million.

In 1935 a geographer named Hu Huanyong drew a diagonal line across the map, from Heihe on the Russian border in the northeast to Tengchong near Myanmar in the southwest. People now call it the Hu Line. About 94% of China's people live east of it, on only about 43% of the land. China's population has nearly tripled since 1935, and that split has barely moved.

Why? The west is mostly mountains, desert and high plateau. The east has flat plains, rivers and rain, which is where farms, and later cities, could grow.

Look for it in Hangzhou

Hangzhou is deep inside the crowded east, on the low, wet plain near the coast.

2.3 Plateau, deserts and plains

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau

In the southwest is the highest and largest plateau on Earth. It averages more than 4,500 m (14,800 feet) above sea level. That is taller than Mount Whitney (4,421 m), the highest mountain in the lower 48 US states. China's biggest rivers start here.

The deserts

The Gobi in the north is mostly bare rock and gravel, not sand. Its name comes from a Mongolian word for "waterless place," and its temperature swings from about -40°C in winter to 45°C in summer. The Taklamakan in the far northwest is the sandy one, with dunes 30 to 150 m tall and as little as 10 mm of rain a year.

The plains

The North China Plain in the east is flat, mostly less than 50 m above sea level, and built from mud carried down by rivers. It is one of the most crowded farming regions in the world.

Key numbers
  • 4,500 m average height of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
  • 10 mm of rain a year in parts of the Taklamakan
  • 50 m or less above sea level across most of the North China Plain

2.4 Two great rivers

The Yellow River (5,464 km, or 3,395 miles) crosses the north. Its water holds more mud than any other big river's, about 34 kg in every cubic meter, which is what makes it yellow. Early Chinese civilization grew up along it, and its floods earned it the nickname "China's Sorrow."

The Yangtze (about 6,300 km, or 3,900 miles) is the longest river in Asia and the third longest in the world, after the Nile and the Amazon. It runs from glaciers on the plateau to the sea at Shanghai. Nearly a third of China's people live in its river basin.

Did you know?

The Yangtze, Yellow and Mekong rivers all begin on the same high plateau.

2.5 North versus south

China's north and south are divided by a line that follows the Qinling Mountains and the Huai River, at about 33° north latitude. North of the line it is colder and drier, and people traditionally grow wheat and eat noodles. South of it, it is wetter and warmer, and rice is the main crop.

The line also decided who got heat. In the 1950s, when energy was scarce, the government built city-wide central heating only north of it, for places with about 90 or more days a year averaging 5°C (41°F) or colder. Homes in the south heat with air conditioners that run in reverse.

Look for it in Hangzhou

Hangzhou sits at about 30° N, south of the line. Its January average is 5°C (41°F), so winters can feel damp and chilly indoors.

Check your understanding

  1. Why can the sun be highest in the sky at 3 pm in western China?
    Show answer

    All of China uses Beijing Time, even though the country is wide enough for five time zones. The far west is so far from Beijing that its "sun noon" comes hours later on the clock.

  2. About what share of China's people live east of the Hu Line, and on how much of the land?
    Show answer

    About 94% of the people, on about 43% of the land.

  3. Which river is the longest in Asia?
    Show answer

    The Yangtze, about 6,300 km.

  4. Is Hangzhou north or south of the heating line, and what does that mean for winter?
    Show answer

    South. Buildings did not get city-wide central heating, so people heat rooms one at a time.
